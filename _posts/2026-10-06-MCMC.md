*Technical notes on classical MCMC algorithms.*

## Target Distribution and Metropolis--Hastings

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods construct a Markov chain whose samples
follow a prescribed target distribution $\pi$. The central problem is therefore
to design a transition mechanism that preserves this distribution.

Suppose the target is a Boltzmann distribution with gradient

$$
\pi(x)\propto e^{-U(x)}, \quad g(x)=\nabla_x\log\pi(x)=-\nabla U(x)
$$

and let $M$ be a positive-definite mass matrix. When no non-identity mass matrix
is specified, one may take $M=I$. For a proposal kernel $q(x'\mid x)$, the
Metropolis--Hastings acceptance probability is

$$
\alpha(x'\mid x)
=
\min\left[
1,\,
\frac{\pi(x')q(x\mid x')}
{\pi(x)q(x'\mid x)}
\right]
$$

It satisfies the detailed-balance condition

$$
\pi(x)q(x'\mid x)\alpha(x'\mid x)
=
\pi(x')q(x\mid x')\alpha(x\mid x')
$$

## Random-Walk Metropolis

Random-walk Metropolis (RWM) is a standard baseline method. It uses the Gaussian
proposal

$$
x' = x+\epsilon M^{1/2}\xi,
\qquad
\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)
$$

with transition kernel

$$
q(x'\mid x)=\mathcal N\!\left(x,\,\epsilon^2M\right)
$$

## Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm

The Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) incorporates gradient
information into the proposal:

$$
x'
=
x+\frac{\epsilon^2}{2}Mg(x)+\epsilon M^{1/2}\xi,
\qquad
\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)
$$

Its transition kernel is

$$
q(x'\mid x)
=
\mathcal N\!\left(
x+\frac{\epsilon^2}{2}Mg(x),\,
\epsilon^2M
\right)
$$

Both RWM and MALA use the Metropolis--Hastings acceptance probability defined
above. For RWM, the Gaussian proposal is symmetric, whereas the gradient drift
makes the MALA proposal asymmetric.

## Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

Hybrid Monte Carlo, as proposed in [Duane1987], or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
(HMC), as named in [Neal2011], introduces an auxiliary momentum
$p\sim\mathcal N(0,M)$ and the Hamiltonian

$$
H(x,p)=U(x)+\frac12p^\top M^{-1}p
$$

The method simulates Hamiltonian dynamics,

$$
\mathrm{d}x=M^{-1}p\,\mathrm{d}t,
\quad
\mathrm{d}p=-\nabla U(x)\,\mathrm{d}t
$$

For example, the dynamics can be approximated using $L$ leapfrog steps:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{l+\frac12} &= p_l-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla U(x_l) \\
x_{l+1} &= x_l+\epsilon M^{-1}p_{l+\frac12} \\
p_{l+1} &= p_{l+\frac12}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla U(x_{l+1})
\end{aligned}
$$

If the numerical trajectory maps $(x,p)$ to $(x',p')$, the HMC acceptance
probability is

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{HMC}}
=
\min\left[
1,\,
e^{-H(x',p')+H(x,p)}
\right]
$$

Because leapfrog integration approximately conserves the Hamiltonian, this
acceptance probability can be close to one.

## Generalized Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

Generalized hybrid/Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (GHMC), proposed in
[Horowitz1991] and also called persistent HMC in [Sohl-Dickstein2014], evolves
the phase-space state $(x,p)$. Rather than fully resampling the momentum as in
HMC, it applies partial momentum refreshment (PMR) to approximate kinetic
Langevin dynamics:

$$
p^{\ast}
=
\phi p+\sqrt{1-\phi^2}\,M^{1/2}\eta,
\qquad
\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,I)
$$

Here, $\eta$ is independent of the current momentum, and the hyperparameter
$\phi\in[0,1)$ controls momentum persistence. GHMC reduces to a full momentum
refreshment when $\phi=0$.

---

**Partial momentum refreshment as an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process.** PMR can be
viewed as an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck (OU) process in which
$\phi=e^{-\gamma\epsilon}$ and $\gamma>0$ is the damping coefficient. Consider
the force-free damped diffusion process

$$
\mathrm{d}p_t
=
-\gamma p_t\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\gamma M}\,\mathrm{d}W_t
$$

Since
$\mathrm{d}(e^{\gamma t}p_ {t})
=e^{\gamma t}\sqrt{2\gamma M}\,\mathrm{d}W_ {t}$, integration gives

$$
p_\epsilon
=
e^{-\gamma\epsilon}p_0
+\sqrt{2\gamma M}
\int_0^\epsilon e^{-\gamma(\epsilon-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_s
$$

The stochastic integral is a Gaussian random variable with zero mean and
variance

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}\left(
\sqrt{2\gamma M}
\int_0^\epsilon e^{-\gamma(\epsilon-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_s
\right)
&=
2\gamma M
\int_0^\epsilon e^{-2\gamma(\epsilon-s)}\,\mathrm{d}s \\
&=
M\left(1-e^{-2\gamma\epsilon}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently,

$$
p_\epsilon
=
e^{-\gamma\epsilon}p_0
+\sqrt{1-e^{-2\gamma\epsilon}}\,M^{1/2}\eta
$$

---

After PMR, GHMC simulates Hamiltonian dynamics from $(x,p^{\ast})$, often using
a single leapfrog step. If this numerical update produces $(x',p')$, the
acceptance probability is

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{GHMC}}
=
\min\left[
1,\,
e^{-H(x',p')+H(x,p^{\ast})}
\right]
$$

The state is then updated according to

$$
(x^{\mathrm{new}},p^{\mathrm{new}})
=
\begin{cases}
(x',p'), & \text{if accepted}, \\
(x,-p^{\ast}), & \text{if rejected}
\end{cases}
$$

## Momentum Reversal and Invariance

**Why reverse the momentum after rejection?** The rejection step uses the
$R$-reversibility of the momentum-reversal operator
$R(x,p)=(x,-p)$. Let $z=(x,p)$, and define the extended target density by
$\widetilde{\pi}(z)\propto e^{-H(x,p)}$. The reversal operator has the following
properties:

- **Involution:** $R^2=I$ and $\lvert\det J_ {R}\rvert=1$.
- **Invariance:** Because the Hamiltonian is even in momentum,
  $H(Rz)=H(z)$, and hence $\widetilde{\pi}(Rz)=\widetilde{\pi}(z)$.

Let $\Psi$ denote the leapfrog integration operator. It satisfies:

- **Symplecticity:** $\lvert\det J_ {\Psi}\rvert=1$.
- **Time reversibility, or $R$-reversibility:**

$$
\Psi^{-1} = R \circ \Psi \circ R \quad \iff \quad \Psi(R(\Psi(z))) = R(z)
$$

Consider the deterministic proposal-and-rejection substep of GHMC, with
transition kernel

$$
K(z,A)
=
\alpha(z)\delta_{\Psi(z)}(A)
+\left(1-\alpha(z)\right)\delta_{R(z)}(A)
$$

where the Metropolis acceptance probability is

$$
\alpha(z)
=
\min\left[
1,\,
\frac{\widetilde{\pi}(\Psi(z))}{\widetilde{\pi}(z)}
\right]
$$

If the input state follows $\widetilde{\pi}$, the density arriving at $z'$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Marginal}(z')
={}&
\alpha(\Psi^{-1}(z'))\widetilde{\pi}(\Psi^{-1}(z')) \\
&+
\left(1-\alpha(Rz')\right)\widetilde{\pi}(Rz')
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $\widetilde{\pi}(Rz')=\widetilde{\pi}(z')$, it remains to show that

$$
\alpha(\Psi^{-1}(z'))\widetilde{\pi}(\Psi^{-1}(z'))
=
\alpha(Rz')\widetilde{\pi}(z')
$$

The left-hand side is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Left}
&=
\min\left[
1,\,
\frac{\widetilde{\pi}(z')}
{\widetilde{\pi}(\Psi^{-1}(z'))}
\right]
\widetilde{\pi}(\Psi^{-1}(z')) \\
&=
\min\left[
\widetilde{\pi}(\Psi^{-1}(z')),\,
\widetilde{\pi}(z')
\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

By $R$-reversibility,
$\Psi(Rz')=R(\Psi^{-1}(z'))$. Therefore, the right-hand side is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Right}
&=
\min\left[
1,\,
\frac{\widetilde{\pi}(\Psi(Rz'))}
{\widetilde{\pi}(Rz')}
\right]
\widetilde{\pi}(z') \\
&=
\min\left[
\widetilde{\pi}(\Psi^{-1}(z')),\,
\widetilde{\pi}(z')
\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, the accepted and rejected contributions together preserve the extended
phase-space target.

## Conclusion

Classical MCMC methods differ primarily in how they construct proposals while
preserving a prescribed target distribution. Random-walk Metropolis uses local
Gaussian perturbations, MALA incorporates gradient information, and HMC uses
approximate Hamiltonian dynamics to propose distant states with limited energy
error. GHMC further introduces persistent momentum through partial refreshment
and uses momentum reversal on rejection to preserve the extended phase-space
distribution.
