*Technical notes on the first step of quantum computation for quantum chemistry.*

## References

- [Szabo1996] A. Szabo and N. S. Ostlund, *Modern Quantum Chemistry: Introduction to Advanced Electronic Structure Theory*, Dover Publications, 1996.
- [Seeley2012] J. T. Seeley, M. J. Richard, and P. J. Love, "[The Bravyi–Kitaev transformation for quantum computation of electronic structure](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4768229)," *The Journal of Chemical Physics* **137**, 224109 (2012).
- [McArdle2020] S. McArdle et al., "[Quantum computational chemistry](https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.92.015003)," *Reviews of Modern Physics* **92**, 015003 (2020).

---

To perform quantum-chemistry calculations on a quantum computer, we need to
represent the electronic Hamiltonian using qubit operators. This post follows
the common second-quantized route: express the first-quantized Hamiltonian in a
spin-orbital basis and then map the resulting fermionic operators to Pauli
operators.

## First-Quantized Hamiltonian

Under the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, the first-quantized electronic
Hamiltonian is

$$
\hat H = \sum_{i=1}^N \hat h(i)
+ \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i \neq j}^N v(\mathbf{r}_i, \mathbf{r}_j) 
+ E_{\mathrm{nuc}}
$$

Here, $\hat h(i)$ contains the kinetic energy and electron–nuclear attraction
of electron $i$, $v(\mathbf r_i,\mathbf r_j)$ is the
electron–electron interaction, and $E_{\mathrm{nuc}}$ is the nuclear repulsion
energy.

For a local multiplicative one-body contribution
$\hat U=\sum_{i=1}^Nu(\mathbf r_i)$, we can write

$$
\hat U
=\sum_{i=1}^N\int d\mathbf r\,
\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i)u(\mathbf r)
=\int d\mathbf r\,
\left(\sum_{i=1}^N\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i)\right)u(\mathbf r)
$$

Using the particle-density operator
$\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i=1}^N\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i)$, the
local contribution becomes

$$
\hat U=\int d\mathbf r\,\hat\rho(\mathbf r)u(\mathbf r)
$$

The kinetic-energy operator is differential rather than multiplicative, so it
is retained inside the general one-body operator $\hat h$. The two-body
interaction can be written as

$$
\hat H_{\mathrm{2-body}} 
= \frac{1}{2} \int d\mathbf{r}_1 d\mathbf{r}_2 \, v(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) 
\hat{\rho}_2(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2)
$$

where the two-body density operator is

$$
\hat{\rho}_2(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2) 
= \sum_{i \neq j}^N \delta(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_i) \delta(\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_j)
$$

## Second-Quantized Hamiltonian

We introduce the vacuum state $\ket{0}$, the fermionic creation and
annihilation operators $a_p^\dagger$ and $a_p$, and the field operators
$\hat\psi^\dagger(\mathbf r)$ and $\hat\psi(\mathbf r)$. The fermionic
operators satisfy the canonical anticommutation relations

$$
\{a_p,a_q^\dagger\}=\delta_{pq}, \qquad
\{a_p,a_q\}=\{a_p^\dagger,a_q^\dagger\}=0
$$

Creating a particle in orbital $p$ gives

$$
\ket{p}=a_p^\dagger\ket{0}
$$

Likewise, creating a particle at position $\mathbf r$ gives

$$
\ket{\mathbf r}=\hat\psi^\dagger(\mathbf r)\ket{0}
$$

Define the orbital wavefunction
$\phi_p(\mathbf r)=\langle\mathbf r\vert p\rangle$. Inserting the complete
one-particle basis gives

$$
\ket{\mathbf r}
=\left(\sum_p\ket p\langle p\vert\right)\ket{\mathbf r}
=\sum_p\ket p\langle p\vert\mathbf r\rangle
=\sum_p\phi_p^*(\mathbf r)\ket p
$$

The corresponding field-operator expansions are therefore

$$
\hat\psi^\dagger(\mathbf r)=\sum_p\phi_p^*(\mathbf r)a_p^\dagger,
\qquad
\hat\psi(\mathbf r)=\sum_p\phi_p(\mathbf r)a_p
$$

The one- and two-particle density operators become

$$
\hat\rho(\mathbf r)
=\hat\psi^\dagger(\mathbf r)\hat\psi(\mathbf r),
\qquad
\hat\rho_2(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2)
=\hat\psi^\dagger(\mathbf r_1)\hat\psi^\dagger(\mathbf r_2)
\hat\psi(\mathbf r_2)\hat\psi(\mathbf r_1)
$$

Let $x=(\mathbf r,\sigma)$ denote both the spatial and spin coordinates. The
one- and two-body operators can then be written as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat H_1 &=
\int dx\,\hat\psi^\dagger(x)\hat h(x)\hat\psi(x) \\
\hat H_2 &= \frac12
\iint dx_1dx_2\,
\hat\psi^\dagger(x_1)
\hat\psi^\dagger(x_2)
v(x_1,x_2)
\hat\psi(x_2)
\hat\psi(x_1)
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the field-operator expansions gives

$$
\hat H=
\sum_{pq}h_{pq}a_p^\dagger a_q+
\frac12\sum_{pqrs}h_{pqrs}
a_p^\dagger a_q^\dagger a_r a_s
+E_{\mathrm{nuc}}
$$

where the one- and two-electron integrals are

$$
\begin{aligned}
h_{pq} &\equiv
\int dx\,
\phi_p^\ast(x)
\hat h(x)
\phi_q(x) \\
h_{pqrs} &\equiv
\iint dx_1dx_2\,
\phi_p^\ast(x_1)
\phi_q^\ast(x_2)
\frac{1}{\lvert\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2\rvert}
\phi_r(x_2)
\phi_s(x_1)
\end{aligned}
$$

## Qubit Hamiltonian

For quantum computation, the fermionic Hamiltonian is mapped to a weighted sum
of Pauli strings $P=\bigotimes_iP_i$, where
$P_i\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}$. A Pauli string acts on a computational-basis product state
according to

$$
\left(\bigotimes_iP_i\right)
\left(\bigotimes_i\ket{q_i}\right)
=\bigotimes_i\left(P_i\ket{q_i}\right)
$$

The single-qubit Pauli actions are

$$
\begin{aligned}
I\ket0&=\ket0, & I\ket1&=\ket1 \\
Z\ket0&=\ket0, & Z\ket1&=-\ket1 \\
X\ket0&=\ket1, & X\ket1&=\ket0 \\
Y\ket0&=i\ket1, & Y\ket1&=-i\ket0
\end{aligned}
$$

Useful products include

$$
X^2=Y^2=Z^2=I, \quad XY=iZ, \quad YX=-iZ
$$

We now adopt the Jordan–Wigner transformation, with $\ket0$ representing an
unoccupied spin orbital, $\ket1$ representing an occupied spin orbital, and
orbitals ordered by increasing qubit index:

$$
a_j^\dagger
=\left(\prod_{k<j}Z_k\right)\frac{X_j-iY_j}{2}
$$

$$
a_j
=\left(\prod_{k<j}Z_k\right)\frac{X_j+iY_j}{2}
$$

The direction of the parity string changes if a different qubit-ordering
convention is adopted.

---

### One-body terms

For $p=q$, the number operator is

$$
a_p^\dagger a_p=n_p=\frac{I_p-Z_p}{2}
$$

For $p<q$, an individual off-diagonal operator maps to four Pauli strings:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_p^\dagger a_q
=\frac14\Big(
&X_pZ_{p+1}\cdots Z_{q-1}X_q
+Y_pZ_{p+1}\cdots Z_{q-1}Y_q \\
&+iX_pZ_{p+1}\cdots Z_{q-1}Y_q
-iY_pZ_{p+1}\cdots Z_{q-1}X_q
\Big)
\end{aligned}
$$

When it is combined with its Hermitian conjugate, the imaginary cross terms
cancel:

$$
a_p^\dagger a_q+a_q^\dagger a_p
=\frac12\left(
X_pZ_{p+1}\cdots Z_{q-1}X_q
+Y_pZ_{p+1}\cdots Z_{q-1}Y_q
\right)
$$

### Two-body terms

If $p,q,r,s$ are all distinct, a double-excitation monomial
$a_p^\dagger a_q^\dagger a_r a_s$ expands into 16 Pauli strings. For a real
coefficient, combining the term with its Hermitian conjugate cancels half of
these strings, leaving eight.

Repeated creation or annihilation indices give zero because
$(a_p^\dagger)^2=a_p^2=0$. Other repeated-index patterns reduce to simpler
operators. For example, when $r=q$ and $s=p$,

$$
a_p^\dagger a_q^\dagger a_q a_p
=n_pn_q
=\left(\frac{I-Z_p}{2}\right)
\left(\frac{I-Z_q}{2}\right)
$$

This density–density term contains four Pauli strings after expansion. As an
example of a conditional single excitation, for distinct $p$, $q$, and $s$,

$$
a_p^\dagger a_q^\dagger a_q a_s
=n_q a_p^\dagger a_s
$$

Pairing this term with its Hermitian conjugate gives

$$
n_q\left(a_p^\dagger a_s+a_s^\dagger a_p\right)
$$

The number operator contributes two Pauli terms, while the Hermitian hopping
operator contributes two, producing at most four products before
simplification.

---

After all fermionic terms have been mapped and like terms have been collected,
the qubit Hamiltonian is a weighted sum of Pauli strings.

For example,

$$
\hat H=c_0I+c_1Z_0+c_2Z_0Z_1+c_3X_0X_1
$$

For a parameterized state $\ket{\psi(\theta)}$, its energy is

$$
E(\theta)=\langle\psi(\theta)\vert\hat H\vert\psi(\theta)\rangle
$$

It can therefore be estimated by measuring the Pauli-string expectation
values:

$$
E(\theta)
=
c_0
+c_1\langle Z_0\rangle
+c_2\langle Z_0Z_1\rangle
+c_3\langle X_0X_1\rangle
$$

## Conclusion

The electronic-structure Hamiltonian can be expressed in a spin-orbital basis
using fermionic creation and annihilation operators and then mapped to a
weighted sum of Pauli strings through a fermion-to-qubit transformation such
as Jordan–Wigner. This qubit Hamiltonian makes its expectation value accessible
through Pauli measurements, providing the connection between quantum chemistry
and variational quantum algorithms.
